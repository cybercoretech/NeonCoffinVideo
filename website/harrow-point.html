01 / THE TOWN

A PLACE THAT APPEARED.

The oldest surviving record is dated 1821. It describes Harrow Point as an established settlement with a harbor, a hill road, a town meeting, and a printing press. There is no founding document. The first page begins halfway through a sentence, as if the town had been there for years before anyone thought to write it down.

People do not move to Harrow Point. They arrive. A traveler takes the old coast road and finds the town at dusk. A ship enters the cove during a storm. Someone steps off a bus that was never scheduled to stop. By morning, the Undertow has printed a name, an occupation, and a place to report for work. Ask the residents how they came here and most will offer a different story—or remember no arrival at all.

Harrow Point has no resident children or teenagers. Young faces at the pier are visitors who eventually leave, unless they do not. The one person the town openly calls young is the October Stranger: a 27-year-old drifter who appeared beside the old sawmill during the annual October Town Festival. Nobody saw him arrive. The Sunday paper already had a blank line waiting for his name.

Locals keep their roles and know their places. The constable watches the coast road. The pressman prints the Sunday notices. The Harrows maintain the house on the hill. At the pier, the lights come on when the carnival is ready. Outsiders who pass nearby rarely speak of Harrow Point afterward. The ones who do tend to disagree about which direction the town was.

03 / PLACES TO KEEP TO

THE TOWN’S FIXED POINTS.

The oldest map in the Town Hall is the surveyor’s plan of 1821, the year the town first appears in the records. Every lot on it was already assigned, including some that would not be built for another hundred years. Open a numbered lot, or a file below, for its full record. Each lot’s survey note is worth reading closely, and in order.

🛋️LOT I / 1821 SURVEY

THE WAKING ROOM

An empty house on Founders’ Row with one green sofa and a television that has never been plugged in. Travelers wake up here, with no memory of the road.

⚓LOT II / 1821 SURVEY

THE OLD HARBOR

The harbor bell sometimes sounds from below the water. Captain Rook’s handmade ship is tied at the far wharf. Bosun watches the tide and never barks at Vesper House.

⚰️LOT III / 1821 SURVEY

LOW TIDE MORTUARY

The town’s old mortuary sits where the coast road bends from the water. A cellar door opens at the lowest tide. The Mortician says the house tells him when the dead arrive.

🎠LOT IV / 1821 SURVEY

THE AMUSEMENT PIER

It opened in 1951 and burned in 1979. Some nights the music plays after midnight. The old midway keeps running, and the carnival’s last ride is never announced twice.

🪦LOT V / 1821 SURVEY

WIDOW’S RISE BURIAL GROUND

The old cemetery on the hill overlooks the town and dark water. Along the north wall are dated stones with no one beneath them. A lantern appears when a boat is overdue.

🏚️LOT VI / 1821 SURVEY

VESPER HOUSE

At the top of Blackthorn Hill, the road ends at the Harrow mansion. Creatures move through the grounds and the dense Black Pines around it. Aurelian Harrow is not known to have crossed the gate.

🌲LOT VII / 1821 SURVEY

THE SAWMILL ROAD

The old sawmill stands just north of town. At dusk during the October Town Festival, a stranger appeared beside it, already carrying the stillness of someone who had been waiting.

🛏️LOT VIII / 1821 SURVEY

THE WAYFARER’S REST

The old coaching inn where the coast road enters the village. Rooms by the night, supper you don’t remember ordering, and a register with no column for departures.

☕LOT IX / 1821 SURVEY

THE GRAVEYARD SHIFT

A chrome railcar diner on Quay Street, open all night since 1953. After every funeral the mourners come for coffee and pie, and so does the deceased.

🏛️NO LOT NUMBER / 1821 SURVEY

THE MAYOR’S HOUSE

A white Federal house on Meeting House Square, between the church and the Town Hall, with Dr. Abbot’s guest cottage at the back. The only building on the 1821 survey without a lot number.
